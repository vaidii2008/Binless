# Model card

## What the model does

Binless forecasts how many units of each fresh product a shop will sell tomorrow. Instead of one number it gives a range: a set of quantile forecasts from the 10th to the 95th percentile. An order quantity is then read off that range by the newsvendor rule in `forecasting/ordering.py`.

The model is LightGBM, trained once per quantile with the quantile objective. One global model learns from every product at once. Each product's sales are divided by its own mean over the previous 28 days, so a product selling 5 a day and one selling 50 a day look alike to the model, and forecasts are scaled back to units afterwards. Forecasts for one product are sorted so that a higher quantile is never below a lower one.

The features are sales 1, 7 and 14 days earlier, the mean of the last 7 days, the variation over the last 28 days, the day of the week, the product category, the discount, promotion and holiday flags, and the weather (rain, temperature, humidity and wind). Every lag and rolling figure uses only days before the forecast date (`forecasting/features.py`).

The settings are untuned starting values: learning rate 0.05, 31 leaves, at least 50 rows per leaf and 300 rounds.

## Where it runs

- For the benchmark, the models train on FreshRetailNet-50K, a public dataset of real fresh-food sales (CC BY 4.0). Its IDs are encoded and its sales normalised.
- For the live app, the models train on 180 days of a simulated shop generated by `forecasting/simulation.py`. The forecasts on the order page are forecasts for that simulated shop only.

## How I evaluated it

All figures come from runs of the scripts named in the README.

- Rolling-origin backtest on the last four weeks of a 50-store sample, retraining before each week: WAPE 0.319 for the median forecast, against 0.347 for a 7-day moving average and 0.426 for the same day last week. Bias -2.5%.
- Ordering decisions in the same backtest, on fully stocked days: ordering the median forecast wasted 14.6% of units ordered against 20.6% for reordering last week's sales, and served 83.7% of demand against 78.2%.
- One run on the dataset's held-out evaluation week, across all 898 stores, under the dataset authors' protocol: WAPE 31.57% on stockout-free days, against 31.56% to 31.97% for the authors' models trained on recorded sales, and 29.02% for their best model trained on demand recovered by a learned model. I touched the held-out week once and changed nothing because of it.

## Where it falls short

- On the held-out week the median forecast is biased low, at -10.13%.
- The upper quantiles cover too rarely: sales were at or below the 80th percentile on 76.8% of backtest days and at or below the 90th on 86.7%.
- Recorded sales understate demand whenever a product sells out, which happened on 44.1% of store-product days in the sample. The models learn from recorded sales. My stockout correction probably overstates lost demand, so the deployed models don't use it.
- The backtests feed in each day's actual weather. A real forecast would use a weather forecast, so real accuracy would be somewhat lower.
- The reasons shown next to each order come from a separate mean-regression model with the same features, because LightGBM's per-feature contributions don't add up for the quantile objective. They describe what drives the typical forecast, not each quantile exactly.
- The live shop's models are trained once and reused nightly, so they don't learn from new days until I retrain and deploy a new version.
- Nothing has been tested on a real shop's own sales records yet.
